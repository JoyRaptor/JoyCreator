Image Skew & Warp — four ways we could build it

A look at what the app can already do today, how the best touch apps handle this, and four honestly-different designs to pick from before we write the spec.

"Two new features we need: an image SKEW and an image WARP… Give me some OPTIONS to choose from… I want to be as clever as possible about consolidating these in a way that's really easy and intuitive to access."

First — the three words, in pictures

These get used interchangeably and they are not the same thing. Each one is a different amount of work for us, so it is worth being precise.

Skew — "lean it" Opposite edges stay parallel. Like italic text, or a shadow falling sideways. Cheap and exact.
Perspective — "lay it down" Far edge gets smaller, near edge gets bigger. Like a poster on a wall you're standing beside, or a phone screen tilting away.
Warp — "bend it" Straight lines become curves. Like cloth, a flag, or a label wrapped round a bottle. This is the expensive one.

What the app can honestly do today

Your instinct that "the plumbing is all there" is half right, and the half that isn't is worth knowing before we choose a design.

Corner-pin: already possible Android's own drawing maths (Matrix) does full four-corner perspective for free. Nothing new to invent. The final exported video path just calls it.
Bendy mesh: the drawing exists The Avatar puppet already bends artwork with a real mesh (drawBitmapMesh) and it runs on the phone's graphics chip. The picture-bending primitive is proven in this app.
On-screen dot handles: exist The preview already has a shared system for draggable dots with tether lines, one-tap undo, and no fighting between layers (used by the gradient curve editor).
The live preview is the catch On screen, an image overlay is drawn by a plain Android image widget. Those can rotate and scale but physically cannot skew or corner-pin. This needs replacing with a custom drawing surface — that is the real work item.
Avatar warp is a ribbon, not a grid The existing warp bends a strip along a bone chain — two columns wide. A photo needs a proper grid. Same drawing call, new maths.
Keyframing a mesh is the real cost Every animated value in the app is a single number on its own track. Four corners = 8 numbers, totally fine. A 5×5 bendy grid = 50 numbers per image. That needs a new way to store and scrub animation, not just a new handle.
FeatureWhere it standsWhat it needs
Flip / mirrorAlready there Negative scale. Nothing.
Skew (lean)Small addition Two extra numbers on the existing transform. Export path is a one-line change; preview needs the custom drawing surface.
Perspective / corner pinSmall addition Eight numbers (four corners). Export is free — the maths is built into Android. Same preview surface as skew, so they cost the same to build once.
Bendy mesh warpReal new pipeline New grid maths, a new way to store dozens of animated points, new handle rules, and a decision about what happens when a warped image also has effects or a mask on it.
Warp as a video "effect" insteadDead end The effects system has a slot that could bend pixels — but effects can only move pixels inside the image's existing rectangle. A corner pulled outward would simply be cut off. Warp has to be part of the object's shape, not an effect on it.

The single most important consequence: skew and perspective are essentially the same feature and cost one build. Bendy warp is a second, much larger project. Every option below is really a question of how gracefully we can ship the cheap half first and leave a door open for the expensive half.

What the best touch apps actually do

Photoshop on iPad One bounding box, always the same four corner handles. A little row of mode icons — Scale / Skew / Perspective / Distort — changes what dragging a corner means. Nothing on the image itself changes appearance.
Procreate Modes live in a bottom bar (Freeform, Uniform, Distort, Warp). Warp puts a net over the image; a separate Advanced Mesh button subdivides that net when you want finer control. Subdivision is opt-in, never the default.
After Effects Keeps them deliberately separate: Corner Pin for hard-surface perspective (clean, four points), Puppet Pin for organic bending (messy, many points). Professionals do not want one tool that does both — the results look wrong in each other's territory.
Alight Motion The closest phone analogue. Corner handles on canvas for fast placement, plus typed number fields for precision, plus long-press nudging for fine work. Three ways in to the same value.
Touch rule #1 Handles need roughly a fingertip of space — 44–48dp. On a phone-sized image, four corners plus four edges plus a rotate stalk is already crowded. A 5×5 mesh is 25 dots and they will overlap.
Touch rule #2 Your finger covers the thing you're dragging. Every serious app either offsets the grabbed point above the finger, or shows a magnified loupe elsewhere on screen. We already have the pattern for this in the mask editor.

The four options

All four assume the same underlying data. They differ entirely in how you get at it with a thumb.

OPTION A

Explicit Modes

A row of mode buttons under the preview. Same four corners; the mode decides what a drag does. The Photoshop-on-iPad approach.

Handles

  • Corners — meaning changes per mode: resize / free-drag / perspective pull.
  • Edges — only appear in Skew. Sliding one leans the image that way.
  • Rotate stalk — stays as it is today, in every mode.

Getting in and out

  • Tap the image, tap the mode. Tap the same mode again to drop back to Move.
  • The mode strip lives in the object drawer, so nothing new floats over the video.

Keyframing

  • One diamond for the whole shape — press it and the current lean/perspective is pinned to that moment. Exactly how Position and Scale already work.
Pros Impossible to trigger by accident. Every mode is discoverable by reading four words. Matches what people already know from Photoshop and Procreate.
Cons Four taps to get anywhere. You cannot skew and perspective in one gesture. The strip is real estate on an already busy drawer, and Warp sits in it as a promise we may not keep for a while.
Build cost Low for Move/Skew/Perspective — one new drawing surface for the preview, plus eight numbers on the object. The Warp button is the expensive one and would ship greyed out or later.
OPTION B

One Smart Widget

No modes at all. Every handle already means something different, and a long-press on a corner pops a little ring to change its behaviour.

Handles

  • Round corners — drag one and only that corner moves. Free distort by default.
  • Little teal bars on the edges — slide one and the image leans (skew). They read as a different shape, so you never confuse them with corners.
  • Long-press any corner — a small ring appears with four choices: size, free, perspective, bend. Whatever you pick becomes that corner's job.

Getting in and out

  • There is no "in". It is simply what the selection box looks like once the feature exists. Double-tap the box to reset the shape.

Keyframing

  • Same single diamond. Because you never left a mode, one diamond covers the whole shape regardless of which handles you used.
Pros Fastest possible workflow — no mode hunting, everything is one gesture away. Nothing added to the drawer. Feels genuinely clever and modern.
Cons Nothing on screen tells you the ring exists. Users will drag a corner meaning "resize" and get a distorted picture instead, which reads as a bug. Eight handles on a phone-sized image is tight, and it collides with the existing pinch-to-scale gesture.
Build cost Medium. The transform maths is the same as A, but the touch layer gets meaningfully harder: hidden gestures, a new popup widget, per-corner state, and a lot of care so the old pinch and rotate behaviours don't break.
OPTION C · RECOMMENDED

Corners First, Grid Later

One new button: Shape. It puts four pins on the image. Pull them anywhere. If you need bending, a + Finer button splits the pins into a net — and that stays optional forever.

Handles

  • Four big round pins, one per corner, and that is the whole idea. Drag one wherever you like. Lean, tilt and perspective all fall out of moving corners — there is nothing else to learn.
  • Keep square toggle — locks the pins so the shape can only lean, never taper. That is skew, without the word.
  • + Finer — splits four pins into nine, then twenty-five. Only then do you get real bending. Press it only if you need it.
  • A small loupe follows the dragged pin, so your thumb never hides the corner you're placing.

Getting in and out

  • One tile in the object drawer: Shape. Tap it on, pins appear over the video. Tap it off, the box goes back to normal. Long-press it to reset.

Keyframing

  • One diamond covers all four pins at once, so a "corner pin animates in" is a single press at each end. Same diamond, same easing menu as everything else in the app.
  • At the finer grid levels the diamond still covers the whole net — you never key one dot at a time, which is what makes it survivable.
Pros One concept — "pull the corners" — covers three features people normally have to learn separately. Nothing is hidden, and nothing new floats over the video until you ask. The cheap half ships as a complete, satisfying feature on its own, and the expensive half slots in behind one button without redesigning anything.
Cons Doesn't offer a numeric "skew 12°" field for someone who wants exactness (we can add typed values later). "+ Finer" only becomes real when we build the mesh, so at launch it either isn't there or is clearly marked coming.
Build cost Lowest of the four for what it delivers. The pins reuse the existing draggable-dot system in the preview almost as-is. The four-corner maths is built into Android and the export path needs a few lines. The one real job is the custom drawing surface for the live preview — which every option on this page needs anyway.
Ships useful in one pass
OPTION D

The Shape Rail

A slim vertical rail floats beside the image while it's selected. Icons for lean, tilt and bend, plus a grid density stepper and the keyframe diamond — all in one strip.

Handles

  • Big dots = corners, small dots = mesh points. Size alone tells you which is which, so you can't grab the wrong one by accident.
  • The rail's top three icons pick lean / tilt / bend; the 4×4 and + buttons change how fine the net is, live.
  • The diamond at the bottom of the rail is right where your thumb already is.

Getting in and out

  • The rail simply appears when an image is selected and slides away when it isn't. No mode to remember entering.

Keyframing

  • The most powerful and the most dangerous: each mesh point can hold its own keyframes, which is how you'd animate a flag rippling — and also how a project file gets enormous and a timeline becomes unreadable.
Pros The most capable of the four by a distance, and everything is one thumb-reach away without covering the picture. Genuinely competitive with desktop tools.
Cons This is the full feature on day one — there is no cheap half to ship first. A floating rail over the video is new furniture that has to work in portrait, landscape and fullscreen. And a 16-point animated mesh is a real risk to file size, undo, and export speed.
Build cost Highest. Needs the new preview surface, the grid maths, a new multi-point animation store, a floating overlay that behaves in every orientation, and answers for how a bent image interacts with effects, masks and blend modes. Realistically two to three times Option C.

Recommendation

Option C — Corners first, grid later.

Four draggable pins is the smallest idea that delivers the largest share of what you asked for. Leaning, tilting and full perspective are not really three features — they are three results of moving corners, and giving people one honest control instead of three named modes is the clever consolidation you were after. It also happens to be the one the app is already built for: Android's own drawing maths does four-corner perspective natively, the export path is a handful of lines, and the preview already has a shared system for draggable dots with proper undo. The Keep square toggle gives you exact skew without ever using the word, and + Finer is a door we can leave in the wall and walk through when a real bendy mesh is worth the money.

One thing to be clear-eyed about: bendy mesh warp is genuinely a separate project, not a finishing touch. The bending itself is proven — the Avatar puppet already does it — but animating dozens of points needs a new way to store and scrub keyframes, and it changes how a warped image has to interact with effects, masks and blend modes. Corner pins are weeks; a keyframable mesh is a lane of its own. Shipping C's pins first means you get the feature people actually reach for most, and we decide about the mesh with real usage behind us.

If you want more than C: take C and borrow one thing from B — small teal bars on the edges for a pure lean. It's a modest addition on top of the pins and it makes skew a single gesture. I would not take B whole: a corner that resizes on Tuesday and distorts on Wednesday will be reported as a bug.

Grounded in: the preview handle system, the image export drawing path, the keyframe tracks, the Avatar puppet's mesh bending, and the effects system's coordinate-remap slot. Interaction references: Photoshop on iPad, Procreate, After Effects Corner Pin / Puppet Pin, Alight Motion.
