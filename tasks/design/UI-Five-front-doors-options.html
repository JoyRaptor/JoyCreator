Five Front Doors

Five different theories of what a lobby is — not five skins of one layout. Same language, same inventory, same 25 destinations. What changes is what organises the screen. Every concept carries a manifest showing where all 25 landed, so nothing quietly evaporates.

The Floor Plan

Your mansion, literally. Rooms are tiles; size means importance and position never changes, so after a week your thumb knows where Studio is without reading. The lobby is the navigation — there is no nav bar under it.

What it's best at

Everything is one tap. No question to answer, no second tier, no nav bar stealing 80px. Muscle memory forms fast because nothing ever moves. It also scales honestly — the drawing engine gets a tile, and you can see it cost something.

The quiet win

The Studio tile carries your last project's name and thumbnail, so the most common action in the app — "keep going" — is the biggest target on screen without the lobby being about projects.

What it costs

A grid of tiles is a well-worn shape; without the illustration work it can read as a launcher. It needs real art to stop looking generic — which is the one thing you can make and I can't.

Where it strains

Eleven tiles is near the ceiling. A twelfth room means re-cutting the grid, and small tiles at the bottom get treated as second-class whether or not they are.

The Marquee

The app asks the question you were already asking. Verbs, not rooms — Record, Edit, Animate, Listen, Find, Ask — and the answer list underneath rewrites itself. Nobody has to learn where anything lives, because nothing has a place; it has a purpose.

What it's best at

It teaches the app. A twelve-year-old and a stranger both understand it in four seconds, and neither has to know the word "Faditor" ever existed. It is also the only concept where Animate gets equal billing with Edit — which matches what you actually build.

The quiet win

Verbs absorb new features without a redesign. Live-avatar streaming is a new line under Record, not a new tile and not a new argument about the grid.

What it costs

Two taps to almost everything, forever. On day 400 you still answer a question you answered on day 1. That is a real tax on the person who uses it daily — you.

Where it strains

Library, Gadgets and Settings are nouns and don't fit the grammar, so they sit in a footer strip and look bolted on. And "Edit" pointing at one giant room hides how much is in there.

The Bench

The lobby is your work in progress. You open the app and the thing you were making is already there, scrubbable, one tap from carrying on. Tools are reached through the work rather than beside it — the way a music studio opens the session, not the instrument cupboard.

What it's best at

Fastest possible resume. For a two-hour documentary edited in fifteen-minute fragments around childcare, this is the only concept that respects that the app is opened forty times per project. One tap, exactly where you left it.

The quiet win

The hero's lane stripes are the real colours from the real project, so you recognise the session before you read its name. "Your things" quietly makes the Workshop a first-class idea without spending a tile on it.

What it costs

Day one is empty. A stranger opens it to a placeholder and an invitation, which is the worst first impression of the five. It needs the demo project to exist before it can ship.

Where it strains

Capture gets demoted to a button on a row — and Capture is currently the entire app. That is either the correction you wanted or a step too far, and it's worth being deliberate about which.

The Thumb Deck

Built around the fact that you use this one-handed, holding a baby. The bottom third is a reachable arc — every room sits inside a thumb sweep, the primary action sits at the natural resting point, and the top two thirds are pure content you never have to reach into. The deck slides away when you want the screen.

What it's best at

Nobody else on Android does this. Every destination is inside one thumb sweep on a 6.7-inch phone, which is a genuine differentiator and directly serves the way you actually work. The big disc is Studio because Studio is what you open.

The quiet win

Long-press fans a disc into its sources without leaving the deck — hold Capture and camera, screen, voice, both and remote arc out under your thumb. That is one tap plus a hold to any capture mode, beating every other concept.

What it costs

Radial menus have a bad reputation for a reason — they are hard to label, hard to hit accurately, and unfamiliar. This is the highest-risk concept here and the one most likely to need two rounds of tuning on a real phone.

Where it strains

It spends a third of the screen on navigation, which the Floor Plan spends on rooms and the Bench spends on your work. It also has to mirror for left-handed use, doubling the layout work.

The Stage

The screen is always showing something real — the live camera, your last frame, your newest export — and the controls float over it in frosted glass. This is your "you never left the thing you're building" made into the whole app rather than just its dialogs. The lobby is a viewport with a dock.

What it's best at

It is the only concept that is never abstract. There is always a picture — yours — and the chrome is a thin frosted layer on top of it. It is also the most photogenic by a distance, which matters for store screenshots and a demo video.

The quiet win

Switching rooms changes what the viewport shows rather than replacing the screen, so the app feels like one continuous place instead of six destinations. That is closer to what you described than any of the others.

What it costs

Frosted glass over moving video is the hardest thing on this page to keep legible, and it costs GPU on a phone that is also encoding. It needs a solid fallback and a "quiet the background" switch, which is the same toggle you already asked for on drawers.

Where it strains

Six dock slots and a "More" is a nav bar wearing a nicer coat — Gadgets, Settings, Sound and Remote all end up behind that ⋯. And keeping a camera warm on the lobby costs battery you may want for a two-hour record.

If you want one opinion

You said you might combine parts, so here is where I think the seams are — the three pieces that would still be right whichever shell wins.

The Floor Plan's geography Fixed positions, size means importance, no nav bar. It's the only one where nothing ever moves, and that is what makes a thumb fast. Start here.
The Bench's hero Put the Bench's real project card — thumbnail, lane colours, Carry on — into the Floor Plan's big Studio tile. You get one-tap resume without giving up the map.
The Deck's hold gesture Hold a tile to fan out its sources. Cheap to add, keeps every tile one tap, and makes seven capture modes reachable without a second screen.
What I'd leave The Marquee's question — lovely for a stranger, a tax on you forever. Consider it for first run only: ask once, then never again.
What I'd test before trusting The Stage. It is the most beautiful and the most expensive, and the frosted-over-video legibility question can only be answered on a real phone with real footage.
The swap you floated Capture ↔ Workshop colours. Try it in the swapper. My read: pink belongs to Capture because pink already means "live" in the Studio, and violet suits a room about characters.
